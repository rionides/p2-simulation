# Analysis

In general, I think that this was a convincing replication of the argument in the original paper. My figure 01 came quite close to the figure in the original paper, although only after a fair amount of optimization and rewriting the code a few times in a fit of perfectionism.  In general, these results confirmed that recovery rises sharply near θ = 1 and generally becomes "sharper"/rises faster as dimension increases. Every curve that was plotted (in the regime that matched the original paper) first reached 50% recovery at a sampled θ of 0.9 or 1.1, and recovery at θ = 2.3 was between 97.5% and 100%, which is pretty close (eyeballing it) to what was described in the Wainwright paper. Obviously, there is no git repo to pull from the original paper to compare with directly, and in any case small differences between curves are expected with 200 trials per condition, but on the whole I think that this is a convincing replication. 

There is a bit of a lack of competing methods in the original paper, but even so I don't think that that's a serious flaw given the paper's specific goals. Some past methods were discussed, but weren't brought up empirically, so I chose to exclude those as well. 


I spent a fair amount  of time on extending this simulation to the weak variable case, and there were indeed some pretty surprising results there. With independent predictors, sublinear sparsity, p = 128, and θ = 1.3, changing five active magnitudes from 1 to 0.25 dropped recovery from 82% to 1%. Even at θ = 2.3, recovery was only 26%, compared with 98% at baseline. That is a substantial change in the results while keeping the paper's Gaussian model, covariance, and noise level. It makes the threshold much less reassuring as a guide to performance at these sample sizes. The theory allows unequal effects and concerns a large-sample limit, so this result fits within its assumptions, but I think this demonstrates in practice that the assumptions that we make when claiming something is sparse can (a) be kind of arbitrary and (b) overlook a lot of real-life behaviour (sparse data is also often enough weak in signal, after all).

It was surprising that this was so tied to p, though.  At p = 512 with linear sparsity and θ = 1.3, for example, both studies recovered correctly in 94.5% of trials. The weak signal-effect was essentially overpowered by more observations and a smaller penalty than the smaller sublinear setting. I will say that the way I designed the extension also has some weakness in the fact that five coefficients represent different fractions of the active variables across conditions.

Also, I made several more figures than in the original paper. The original Figure 2 was informative enough, but it also reflects in my opinion the relative difficulty of running a simulation study in 2006 vs 2026. Since I had so much more compute available to me, I used much more of it, and made larger and more figures as a result. The original Figure 2 curves were also a bit crowded and don't include the use of the Toeplitz effects that the paper mentions, so I made a figure for each kind of covariance (e.g. the [Toeplitz plot](results/figures/02_recovery_toeplitz.pdf)) which uses nine panels, separating the regimes and dimensions and comparing baseline with weak signals in each panel. These figures show qualitative agreement with the original experiment, plus they add an extra comparison the paper did not make (and to boot they have error bars, which I felt were very sorely lacking in the original).

I also thought that the addition of the [failure diagnostic plot](results/figures/03_failure_modes.pdf) was an interesting source of extra information on where exactly the failure to recover was coming from. In a future project, I would expand from showing only θ = 1.3 to try and even generalize a little bit about the failure patterns here. I am not enough of an expert on high-dimensional statistics to figure out exactly what I would do with that information, though. 

Also, if I were to be even more of a perfectionist about this, the first change I would make is to vary both weak-effect magnitude and weak-effect proportion, to try and get a clearer answer about when the threshold becomes useful in practice. I would keep the Gaussian model initially so that interpretation stays close to the paper, and also sample θ more finely. I would also probably compare the paper's penalty with cross-validated Lasso to see what changes when k is unknown (in practice, an analyst wouldn't know k at all, which is another one of those assumptions that make theory easier but applications to real life more difficult).
